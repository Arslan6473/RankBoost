To calculate reading time, divide the number of words in your text by an average reading speed in words per minute (WPM). A common default for online prose is around 225 WPM, so a 1,350-word article takes roughly six minutes to read. Adjust the speed down for technical content and up for light, skimmable writing.

## The reading time formula

The formula is straightforward:

**Reading time in minutes = total words ÷ words per minute**

For example:

| Word count | At 200 WPM | At 225 WPM | At 250 WPM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 words | 2.5 min | 2.2 min | 2 min |
| 1,000 words | 5 min | 4.4 min | 4 min |
| 1,500 words | 7.5 min | 6.7 min | 6 min |
| 2,500 words | 12.5 min | 11.1 min | 10 min |

Most sites round the result to the nearest whole minute and show it as "5 min read".

## Why reading time matters

An estimated reading time helps readers decide whether to read now, save the article for later or skim. It sets expectations, and it is a small signal that you respect their time.

For writers and editors, reading time is also a planning tool. It is easier for many people to think about "a four-minute read" than "a 900-word article", especially when planning newsletters, onboarding docs or content for busy audiences.

## Choosing a reading speed

Average adult reading speed for ordinary prose is commonly cited at around 200 to 250 words per minute. The right number for your estimate depends on what you write and who reads it:

- **Light content** such as listicles, news and casual blog posts can use a higher figure, around 250 WPM or more.
- **General web articles** are well served by a middle value around 225 WPM.
- **Technical, legal or academic content** is read more slowly, so a figure closer to 150 to 200 WPM is more realistic.
- **Content with code, tables or images** takes longer than its word count suggests, so consider padding the estimate.

Remember that an estimate is just that. Individual readers vary a lot, and many people skim online content rather than reading every word.

## Best practices for displaying reading time

### Put it near the title

Readers decide whether to commit in the first few seconds. Showing the estimate near the headline or byline means they see it at the right moment.

### Round sensibly

"4 min read" is easier to process than "4 minutes 23 seconds". Round to the nearest minute and avoid showing zero for very short posts.

### Be honest

Do not shave minutes off to make an article look quicker. If a guide is long, a realistic estimate helps the right readers settle in and lets others bookmark it.

### Recalculate after edits

Reading time drifts as drafts change. Recheck the estimate before publishing, especially after large edits.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the wrong text.** Exclude navigation, sidebars, comments and author bios. Paste only the article body.
- **Using one speed for everything.** A dense technical tutorial and a light opinion piece should not use the same rate.
- **Confusing reading with speaking.** People speak much more slowly than they read silently. For scripts and presentations, use the [Speaking Time Calculator](/tools/speaking-time) instead.

## FAQ

### Should images and videos be included in reading time?

The basic formula only counts words. If your post relies heavily on images, charts or embedded video, consider adding time manually so the estimate reflects the full experience.

### Do people actually read the whole article?

Many online readers skim headings and the first lines of paragraphs. A reading time estimate describes reading at a steady pace, so treat it as an upper bound for skimmers.

### Can reading time improve SEO?

Reading time is not a known ranking factor. It can improve the reader's experience by setting clear expectations, which supports the broader goal of creating helpful content. See our [SEO basics guide](/blog/seo-basics) for the fundamentals that matter more.

### What reading speed should I use for children's content?

Younger readers read more slowly than adults, so use a noticeably lower WPM. Test with real readers from your audience if accuracy matters.

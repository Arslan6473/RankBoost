## What a meta title is and why it matters

A meta title (also called a title tag or SEO title) is the HTML element that names a page. Browsers show it in the tab, and search engines usually show it as the blue, clickable headline in search results. It lives in the head section of your page:

```html
<title>Best Running Shoes for 2026 | Acme</title>
```

The meta title matters for two reasons. First, it's one of the clearest signals you can give search engines about what a page covers. Second, it's the most visible part of your search listing, so it has a big influence on whether people click your result or a competitor's.

Google doesn't always display the exact title you write. It may rewrite titles that are too long, stuffed with keywords or don't match the page. Writing a clear, accurate title in the first place is the best way to keep control of how your page appears.

## Meta title length: how long should it be

The commonly recommended range is **50–60 characters**, which usually fits within the roughly 600-pixel width Google gives titles on desktop. Character count is only an approximation, because letters have different widths: a title full of capital W's and M's will get truncated sooner than one with lots of i's and l's.

A few practical rules:

- Aim for 50–60 characters as a default.
- Put the important words first, so a truncated title still makes sense.
- Shorter is fine when it's clear. A 40-character title that says exactly what the page is about is better than a padded 60-character one.

## Meta title best practices

### Lead with the primary keyword

Searchers scan the first few words of each result. Putting your main keyword early confirms relevance at a glance and protects it from truncation.

### Match search intent

The format of your title should match what people want. Someone searching "buy trail shoes" wants a product page, while "how to choose trail shoes" wants a guide. Using a transactional title on an informational page (or vice versa) can hurt clicks even if you rank.

### Make every title unique

Each page on your site should have its own title. Duplicate titles make it harder for search engines to tell pages apart and confuse searchers who see several similar results.

### Use your brand wisely

Adding your brand at the end builds recognition, especially if people already know you. On long titles, it's usually the first thing to drop.

### Write for people, not just algorithms

A title that reads naturally and promises something useful will usually outperform one crammed with keyword variations.

## Common meta title mistakes

- **Keyword stuffing**, such as "Running Shoes, Best Running Shoes, Cheap Running Shoes." It looks spammy and Google may rewrite it.
- **Being vague**, like "Home" or "Products." These titles tell searchers nothing.
- **Overpromising.** A clickbait title that the page doesn't deliver on leads to quick back-clicks.
- **Ignoring truncation.** If the key benefit sits at character 70, most searchers won't see it.
- **Leaving default titles** from your CMS, such as "Untitled page" or the site name alone.

## FAQ

### Is the meta title the same as the H1?

Not necessarily. The meta title appears in search results and browser tabs, while the H1 is the main heading on the page itself. They often overlap, but the title can be tuned for search results, and the H1 can be written for readers who have already arrived.

### Should I include the year in my meta title?

Only if the content is genuinely updated for that year, such as a buying guide or a list of tools. A year can make a result look fresh, but you'll need to keep it current or it will start to look outdated.

### What separator should I use before my brand name?

A pipe or a hyphen are both common and take up little space. Pick one and use it consistently across your site.

### How often should I update my meta titles?

Review them when a page isn't getting the clicks you'd expect for its ranking position, when the content changes significantly, or when search intent for the keyword shifts.
